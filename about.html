01 The long version

From mice, to people, to the systems people have to trust.

the through-line

“Research, I learned, is as much an art as a science.”

Before I studied people, I spent seven years studying mice, specifically neuropeptides and cellular communication pathways, in a research world where experiments came with controls, protocols, and the reassuring assumption that enough rigor would eventually reveal a single root cause.

Later, my research advanced to human subjects. Humans, it turns out, are considerably less cooperative than mice. They'll tell you a task failed because there was no tutorial while skipping the tutorial. Humans are masters of both conscious and unconscious self-deception, and decoding observed behavior requires more than tracing the molecular pathways from DNA to protein expression.

Maybe it's the tough rebel in me, but that complication attracted me: a domain where interpretation, judgment, and empathy matter just as much as methodological rigor.

I got my first real taste of product work at Huawei, designing and researching internal enterprise tools. It was also where I discovered that enterprise software is where good intentions go to become eighteen-step workflows.

At Google, I joined the Google Ads Rapid Research team (REACH-UX) and moved quickly across products including AdMob, Google My Business, Discover, and Shopping. I studied everything from search and decision-making to how people navigate grocery aisles in the real world, clipboards and all. The pace taught me to be methodologically flexible. Sometimes the right answer is a carefully controlled study; sometimes it is getting out of the building and watching what actually happens.

I spent the next four years at Malwarebytes, where I grew from Senior UX Researcher to Research Manager, led a team of five, and worked across both consumer and enterprise cybersecurity. It was a period of major product transformation, including a $100M investment to accelerate the company's consumer and corporate businesses, and my work increasingly moved from evaluating experiences to helping shape the systems behind them.

I led research that helped evolve a legacy enterprise security platform toward a scalable, posture-based experience and unify a fragmented consumer security and privacy portfolio. Along the way, I built research programs, coached researchers and designers, and learned that producing good evidence is only half the job. The other half is helping an organization actually act on it.

And since you've read this far, here's the less predictable part. Remember what I said about research being as much art as science? I took that one step further and trained in acting and screenwriting at Beijing Film Academy. It's the strangest line on my résumé and one of my favorites. Research taught me to observe people; acting taught me another way to understand a perspective from the inside.

only have time for one? Start with the mental model study behind that $100M bet.

Understand the system → explain the mechanism → influence direction early → make the evidence travel.

01

The feature is rarely the whole problem.

I look for the mental model and system underneath the surface issue.

02

What happened isn't enough. I want to know why.

I trace findings until the explanation survives scrutiny.

03

Research as the compass, not the brake.

I invest upstream, while assumptions and direction are still cheap to change.

04

A finding that doesn't travel didn't happen.

Evidence creates value when it reaches the decisions it should change.

04 How I work with people.

Three stances for what it feels like to have me in the room.

01

alignment

I don't confuse alignment with agreement.

My job is to represent the user, even when the evidence is inconvenient. I push back while a decision is still open and stand by the evidence when research needs a voice in the room. Once direction is set, I commit fully to making it work.

02

craft

I compete with the work, not the room.

I'm a let-the-work-speak-for-itself kind of person. When something is good, I look for what would make it exceptional. The pressure is directed at my own craft, not at outperforming the person next to me.

03

people

One person at a time.

Managing a team of five taught me that what unlocks one person can be noise to another. I adjust how I coach, challenge, and support people to the individual in front of me. It's the same instinct I bring to research: the aggregate tells me where to look; the person helps me understand why.
